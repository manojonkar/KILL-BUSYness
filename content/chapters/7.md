# **LEADERSHIP CREATES** 

**Leadership creates. It brings new realities into being: a culture, a standard, a future** that, before the leader chose to build it, existed only as potential. 

The organisations that truly transform are always led by someone who has made this choice. Choosing to stop managing the existing reality and start creating a new one. 

John Kotter’s research at Harvard confirms this: most organisations are overmanaged and under-led. 

Talent isn’t the problem, and neither is management competence. Leadership requires a different orientation: the deliberate choice to create rather than optimise. Organisations rarely name that difference explicitly enough to develop it.

## **7.a Three Comfort Zones Leaders Must Break** 

**Comfort zone one: Past Ways of Working.** 

The method that built you is now limiting you. The processes, habits, and structures that produced the organisation’s early success have now become the walls. The organisation defends the method instead of serving the mission. It has built an immunity to change. 

Robert Kegan and Lisa Lahey’s ‘Immunity to Change’ (2009) describes this pattern in detail. 

Some of the common responses under the corporate immunity to change are: 

1. This is how we have always done it. 

Worth asking: Does past success guarantee future success? 

2. We are too busy right now. 

Worth asking: Is operational urgency truly more important than strategic improvement right now? 

3. We tried this before, and it didn’t work. 

Worth asking: Does one earlier attempt settle the question for good? 

4. The market is different for us. 

Worth asking: Are we certain we are the exception?

5. Our customers haven’t asked for it. 

Worth asking: Can customers always articulate what they will need next? 

6. Let’s form a team to study it. 

Worth asking: Does involving more people always produce a better decision? 

The current system is uncomfortable, but change feels riskier. Organisations often choose familiar inefficiency over unfamiliar improvement. 

## **Comfort Zone Two: Silos and Hierarchy.** 

The hierarchy that provided clarity at an earlier stage now slows decision-making and distances leadership from reality. The silos that increased focus and productivity now prevent the cross-functional collaboration that the next stage demands. 

Production and Sourcing are in a permanent cold war. R&D operates on a different calendar, one where urgency hasn’t yet been invented. Sales sells what doesn’t exist and calls it optimism. Customer Service apologises for what it didn’t cause. Finance tracks numbers that Operations has stopped believing in. They aren’t different departments. They are different organisations, sharing a name, a building, and very little else. The only thing moving freely across these invisible borders is the blame.

The organisation is structured for the company it was, not the company it needs to become. 

A trading company we worked with had built a large, experienced leadership team and a reputation for exceptional client service. For years, their model was the source of their strength: each business unit operated as a separate SBU, with its own leadership, its own client relationships, and its own ego. The autonomy created accountability. It worked well. 

Until it didn’t. When we began working with them, a pattern that nobody had named had been running for years. Multiple SBUs were independently approaching the same clients, each pitching their own product range, each managing their own relationship, each unaware of what the other was doing. The client would receive three calls from the same company in a week, from three different people, for three different products. The internal cost in duplicated effort was significant. The external signal to the client was fractured. The SBU model, which had given the company agility, had also given it invisibility from itself. 

There was a second, deeper problem. The company’s working style was built around serving individual clients with excellence. And they did; their client satisfaction was genuine. But because every relationship was managed individually, the company had never developed the

discipline of working on customer segments. They were equally attentive to every client regardless of strategic fit or scale potential. The result was an organisation that was genuinely loved by many clients but had never built the scale that concentrated focus on high-potential segments would have created. 

The SBU model was not wrong when it was designed. The individual-client approach was not wrong when it was built. Both were exactly right, for the stage of the organisation that then existed. It was a success that had outlived its context. Breaking it required the leadership team to acknowledge that the structure they were proud of was now the ceiling they were bumping against. ## **Comfort Zone Three: Past Success** 

Is your organisation using past success as a _seed_ to grow from, as a foundation to build on, OR 

As a benchmark that has shaped the limited expectations of the future? 

In a recent case of an organisational turnaround, a new CEO of a global organisation launched a large initiative for deep strategic thinking and direction setting. He involved 500 leaders across the organisation in making real choices about where the organisation would play and how it would win. The process was uncomfortable by design.

The outcome was not just a strategy document. It was **500 leaders** who had been inside the creation of a new reality, who understood the choices that had been made and why, and who could carry that understanding into every room the CEO can’t reach. 

Companies that proactively disrupt their own business models before being disrupted externally consistently outperform those that wait. Organisations that break their own paradigms are those that lead. 

The companies that protect their business models, even when the need for change is visible, eventually get broken by someone else. 

## **7.b Creating Time for Creating the Future** 

One of the most practical expressions of the choice to create rather than just manage is the protection of time for creation. 

The Managing Director and a fellow Director of a client company in Karnataka, India, made a deliberate, structural decision: one full day per week, dedicated entirely to creating the future, not managing the routine. 

A leadership team of a Mumbai-based client made a similar commitment and has been progressively moving towards three dedicated days per week, every alternate day, for building the future.

## **7.c You Can’t Delegate the Momentum of Change** 

Every leader who has tried to drive organisational transformation discovers this, usually the hard way. 

**The moment you step back, the organisation drifts back. Steadily, reliably, the gravitational pull of the familiar reclaims the space the leader vacated.** 

You can share the leadership. You can build a team that carries it with you. What you can’t delegate is its energy, not until the culture itself has been transformed. 

## **7.d From Company to Ecosystem** 

Most people build businesses; good leaders build organisations, but the extraordinary ones build ecosystems. 

An ecosystem mindset redraws the boundaries. Customers become co-creators of value, not transactions to be managed. Suppliers and service providers become collaborators in expanding the space rather than adversaries to be defended against.

The question shifts from “how do we win our share of the market?” to “how do we co-create a larger one?” 

An organisation with a healthy ecosystem doesn’t just resist disruption. It is creating disruption to all those companies that are fighting as individual companies. Ecosystems will win against individual companies almost all the time. 

Consider an educational institution that could have remained a well-run school. Instead, it built an ecosystem, franchisees, specialist coaching providers, sports academies, ed-tech organisations, teacher development partnerships, and parent education programmes. Each partnership expanded the institution’s reach without requiring it to own and control everything. The school became a platform. The platform became a movement.

## **7.e The Choice Is Made Every Day** 

The leader’s choice isn’t made in a single dramatic moment of courage. It is made in every meeting, every conversation, every decision about what to accept and what to challenge, whether you ask the creative question or the operational one. Whether you defend the existing process or question whether the process is still serving the mission. 

Every morning, two options appear. 

Option One: Manage the existing reality. Protect what worked before. 

Option Two: Create the new reality. Break the existing paradigm. 

A leader who makes option two daily, not in one dramatic moment, but in a hundred ordinary ones, produces a HighPerformance Organisation.

# **STRATEGY** 

## **Strategy is Business Everything else is BUSYness.** 

As strategy scholar Roger Martin argues, most organisations don't have a strategy. They have a plan. A well-constructed, overly discussed plan with targets, timelines, and everything else. And it sits in a document, or a deck, and it gets referenced in quarterly business reviews, and it gives everyone a comfortable sense that the organisation knows where it's going. 

Strategy is a set of interconnected choices, and a real choice costs you something, by definition. 

Most organisations are unwilling to make that sacrifice. And so, what gets called a strategy is actually a collection

of intentions, things the organisation hopes to do, without the discipline of choosing what it won't do. 

**BUSYness is often the consequence of** **_this_ absence of strategic choice.** 

_When everything is a priority, nothing is_ . When there's no clear answer to “What do we not do?”, every opportunity feels like it should be explored. The organisation spreads itself thin: **_active everywhere, decisive nowhere._** 

## **3.a A Strategy as a Set of Interconnected Choices** 

Inspired by Michael Porter's work, we ask these simple questions for every client company to reflect on: 

1. Which customer segments do you wish to serve? 

2. Which needs of these customer segments do you wish to serve? 

3. How do you serve these needs in a way that gives you a long-term competitive advantage? 

These questions require slowing down, thinking, collecting information, reflecting, discussing, and ultimately making a conscious choice. 

When organisations see the market and customer needs afresh, they can create solutions that may make little sense to others but make a great deal of sense to the target audience, because they are addressing problems their customers have been living with, sometimes without even

## **3.b How BUSYness Hides the Absence of Strategy** 

One of the most dangerous things BUSYness does is hide the absence of strategy. 

When an organisation is in full motion, busy with meetings, projects, budgets and reviews, there's no obvious signal that strategy is absent. 

What you can't see, from inside the web of incessant activity, is whether any of it is actually moving the organisation in sync with its long-term strategy. 

An organisation can be BUSY for years while its competitive position erodes. The market is shifting in a direction the BUSY organisation is too occupied to sense. 

## **3.c The Incumbent's Dilemma** 

Success builds a trap. The very things that made an organisation successful, e.g., its products, its processes, its culture, its relationships, over time become the box that prevents it from becoming greater. 

What worked in the past influences what we believe is true, and what we believe is true becomes the box we can't see our way out of. We create our own limits when we mistake our beliefs for reality.

When Samsung and LG entered India in the late 1990s, the companies had every advantage: established distribution, customer trust, manufacturing expertise, and deep pockets. ## **3.d Long-Term Thinking and Short-Term Thinking** 

If you aren't thinking long-term, you aren't thinking. You are reacting. 

BUSYness produces a very specific leadership cost: shortterm thinking. 

Short-term thinking is the orientation towards this quarter and this financial year. It responds to what's urgent. It optimises for the near term. It underinvests in the capabilities, relationships, and strategic positions that would make the organisation powerful five or ten years from now. 

## **3.e Saying No** 

The most important word in the strategy game is “NO”. 

Saying NO to a lot of good ideas, so that you can say a meaningful YES to one. 

In a BUSY organisation, the word “no” is rarely heard. New initiatives are launched. Old initiatives are never killed. Every opportunity feels like it should be explored. The result is an organisation doing a hundred things adequately rather than ten things exceptionally. 

**High-performance organisations are ruthlessly focused.** 

They make the real choice about whom to serve, what to serve, and how to serve. They stay true to that choice and

Mohit Sharma’s story is one we return to often when leaders say they can’t focus. As a consultant in Maharashtra, Mohit had built a reputation as the go-to expert in lean management. When we first met him, he was running five businesses simultaneously: lean consulting, training programmes, insurance, marketing products, and more. All were underperforming. “I know I’m spread thin,” he admitted. “But I can’t let any of them go. They all have potential.” His lean consulting practice was getting maybe 30 percent of his attention, the very thing that could have made him a national authority in his field. 

Our initial conversations met polite resistance. Mohit could see the logic: focus everything on lean consulting, become a dominant player, and let go of everything else. But the commitment required was real. “I understand what you are saying, but you are asking me to walk away from revenue. Real revenue. Relationships I’ve built over the years. What if I bet everything on lean consulting and it doesn’t work out?” This is what strategic choice actually costs, not money or time, but the willingness to close doors. 

Most leaders, when they reach this moment, retreat. They choose the comfort of doing many things adequately over the terror of doing one thing extraordinarily.

Several weeks later, Mohit called with his decision. “I’m doing it. I’m shutting down everything except lean consulting, handing some over, closing the rest. It’s terrifying. But the choice is made.” 

Unfortunately, fourteen months into the transformation, Mohit mentioned casually, “We're doing this personal transformation training programme. Well, this client specifically asked. It is just a one-time thing.” 

One programme became three. The word “no” was getting harder to say. Growth creates its own gravitational pull towards compromise. Success makes you feel invincible, until it makes you vulnerable again. 

We had a straight conversation: “Mohit, you're doing it again. You're drifting back into being everything to everyone.” The pain in his face was real; he knew it was true. This time, the choice went deeper. Not just lean consulting, but lean consulting for a specific client profile only. The target audience became manufacturing companies above a certain scale. Everything else? Not to be pursued. The discipline required was brutal. 

The results were unambiguous. The business grew 15x in two years, not 15 per cent, fifteen times. Mohit called after closing his largest-ever deal. “This client wouldn’t have taken my call three years ago. Now they’re paying us more for one transformation than I made in an entire year running five businesses.”

That is what becomes inevitable when an organisation stops distributing its capability across everything and concentrates it on the one thing it can do better than anyone else. 

Leaders who have made and sustained this choice consistently describe a counterintuitive experience: as the focus narrows, the trajectory accelerates. 

The three questions at the heart of strategic choice, which customers, which needs, which competitive advantage, are not answered once and set aside. They are returned to at a regular frequency and fine-tuned.

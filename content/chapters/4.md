## **4.a Recruitment Compromised** 

Every hire is a decision about what kind of organisation you’re building. BUSY organisations treat every hire as an operational necessity. High-performance organisations treat every hire as a strategic investment. 

The right person takes time. You don’t have time. So you hire the available candidate instead of the right one. It 

43 

_Kill BUSYness_ 

sounds reasonable at the moment, and that’s the trap, because you rarely get a clean second chance to correct it later. 

Neha Patel, CTO of a forty-person IT services firm in Pune, described the pattern precisely. “We’d bring someone in, they’d look good in interviews, and three months later we’d realise they couldn’t do the job. Then we were back to recruiting while the projects suffered.” 

We asked a different question: what if recruitment were a strategic investment, not an operational necessity? They rebuilt the entire process end to end, from role design through competency-based assessment to a paid work trial, where candidates worked in the real team on the real job before either side made the final call. 

“We lost some candidates who didn’t want to invest the time,” Neha admitted. “But those were exactly the ones we didn’t need. The people who stayed were the ones who wanted to prove they could deliver, and wanted to find out for themselves whether this was a team worth joining.” 

In under two years, the company grew from 40 people to over 300. More importantly, attrition dropped to 8 per cent in an industry where 25 per cent is the norm. Client satisfaction reached its highest point. “We stopped treating recruitment as filling seats and started treating it as building our future,” Neha said. “That single shift changed everything.”

## **4.b Training Compromised** 

The new hire joins. A training programme exists somewhere in the organisational records, but operational demands always take priority. The person learns on the job, which means they learn the existing habits, the existing shortcuts, and everything that actually needs to change. 

Structured onboarding improves new-hire retention by 82% and measurably increases productivity (Glassdoor, 2015). Few organisations build to that standard, and fewer still build it into a system that survives a change in leadership. 

A national telecom company built its early front-line capability on exactly this foundation, structured multiweek programmes for store staff and franchise managers, certified trainers, and supervised in-store coaching. 

Then a new management team arrived. “We can do these training programs in a day. Two days, maximum. Keep costs low.” Multi-week programmes became single-day events. Trainer certification was dropped. In-store coaching stopped. Training still appeared on the calendar, still showed up in the activity reports, still got counted in the dashboard. But it had stopped being a competencybuilding activity.

Franchise partners began complaining that the support they’d been promised wasn’t materialising. The gap between what the brand claimed and what a customer experienced at the front line widened with every passing quarter. 

## **4.c Promotion Compromised** 

The best salesperson becomes the sales manager. The most senior employee becomes the supervisor. The logic seems sound: reward your best performers with advancement, because tenure deserves to be recognised. 

Domain excellence and leadership excellence, though, aren’t the same thing. 

A 2019 study analysed data from 214 companies and 53,035 workers. The finding was stark: companies systematically promote their best salespeople into management roles, even though individual sales performance is negatively correlated with management performance. The better someone is at selling, the research suggests, the worse they’re likely to be at leading a sales team. 

Promoting people into leadership without preparing them for leadership doesn’t reward them. It sets them up to fail. 

This pattern recurs with notable consistency across industries.  One  instance  stays  with  us.  A  plant

He was a gifted engineer; he knew the machines, the materials, and the processes better than anyone on the floor. What nobody asked, what the organisation never paused to examine, was whether he knew how to lead people, whether he could coach someone whose work fell short, hold a standard with a team that was used to doing things a certain way, or demand excellence without undermining the people who had to deliver it.

## **4.d Sales Compromised** 

With a team built on compromise, the sales process follows suit. The wrong clients get pursued and signed, the wrong fit, the wrong margin, the wrong expectations. The team is under pressure to generate revenue, and pressure produces the same thing in sales that it produces 

47 

_Kill BUSYness_ 

everywhere else: short-term optimisation at the expense of long-term health. 

Suresh Reddy, managing director of an auto components manufacturer, called us in frustration. “We’re winning bids left and right, but somehow we’re not making money. I don’t understand it.” We asked to see his win-rate data. The numbers told a brutal story: a 25% win rate by number of bids, but only 5% by value. He was winning a lot of small orders while losing the large, profitable ones, same processing costs, same management overhead, no profit to show for it. 

We picked one “win” at random for a deep analysis: a ₹38.5 lakh order (roughly USD 46,000). On paper, it showed a ₹2.5 lakh profit (about USD 3,000), and Suresh was pleased with it. Then we did the real math, full cost accounting, hidden overheads, time allocation. The celebration turned to silence. The “profitable” order had actually cost the company ₹62,000 (about USD 750). “How many more winning bids are quietly bankrupting us?” Suresh asked, his face pale.

## **4.f Brand Compromised** 

A compromised delivery experience produces a compromised brand, not through a single catastrophic failure, but through the steady accumulation of slightly-

_Kill BUSYness_ 

below-expectation experiences. “Good people, but you have to manage them.” That phrase, you have to manage them, is the dilution of your brand, one sentence at a time. 

**Instead of you solving the client’s problems, you’ve become the problem the client has to solve.** 

Brand damage doesn’t always announce itself loudly. Sometimes it arrives in a single sentence from a procurement officer that closes a door you didn’t realise you’d been slowly, systematically shutting. 

A manufacturing company in a sensitive industry had won a competitive bid, a significant order, the kind that would have opened a new tier of growth. The numbers were right. The price was competitive. They’d earned the contract on paper. Then the client’s procurement team made its final assessment. The pattern they saw in the vendor’s recent history- inconsistent quality, a track record of delivery delays, promises made and revised and revised again- made the decision easy. “Even though you’ve won the bid,” the procurement officer told them, “we’re not in a position to give you the order.” The entire order went to a larger, more reliable competitor. 

That order would have changed the company’s growth trajectory. It was within reach. They had qualified for it on merit. They lost it not to a competitor’s better price or superior product, but to their own compromised operational reputation.

## **4.g Future Compromised** 

There’s no single story for this link, because Future Compromised isn’t a moment you can point to. It’s what every other link adds up to, quietly, over years. 

In a High-Performance Organisation, each link looks different. Recruitment is a strategic investment: the question isn’t “who’s available?” but “who will help us become what we need to become?” Training is sustained, rigorous, and verified, a capability-building system, not a calendar event. Promotion follows demonstrated readiness, not tenure, and always comes with deliberate preparation for the new role. Sales brings in clients the organisation can serve excellently, at margins that let it keep improving. Delivery is so consistent that it becomes the organisation’s most powerful sales tool. Brand is simply the accumulation of hundreds of kept promises. And the organisation’s future is protected by a leadership pipeline that doesn’t depend on any single individual’s continued presence. 

**_An organisation that has compromised recruitment, training, promotion, sales, delivery, and brand hasn’t just underperformed in the present. It has mortgaged its future._** The capabilities it will need to compete tomorrow aren’t being built today, and a chain doesn’t fail at its weakest link. It fails everywhere downstream of its weakest link.

## **4.h Every Manager and Supervisor as the CEO’s Representative** 

Job titles like “supervisor” and “manager” suggest seniority and scope, but they don’t describe the real job. At every level, the role is precise and demanding: to be the CEO’s representative. 

The CEO can’t be everywhere. The supervisors and managers are everywhere. They are the culture. They are the standard. Every decision a manager makes either carries the organisation’s purpose, strategy, and standards further into the world or erodes them. 

A manager must know the strategy being built and the culture being created, and their specific role in both. They must make decisions the CEO would be proud to own. 

That is the standard. And it starts with the CEO holding it first. 

In a mid-sized manufacturing company in Rajasthan, the MD noticed that two of his plant managers were consistently making decisions that prioritised their department’s convenience over the customer’s experience. When he investigated, the root cause was simple: neither manager had ever been told, clearly and specifically, what decisions they were empowered to make and what standard those decisions had to meet. They were managing their departments. They were not representing the MD’s vision. Once the MD held a half-day session

_Kill BUSYness_ 

with all twelve of his managers, not to give instructions, but to share his thinking, his priorities, and the standard he held himself to, something shifted. Within ninety days, cross-department complaints dropped by half. Not because of a new process. Because twelve people finally understood what it meant to lead on his behalf. 

This is what closes the chain. A Competency Chain built link by link still depends on the people running it to see themselves as more than department heads, as the CEO’s representatives, everywhere the CEO cannot be.

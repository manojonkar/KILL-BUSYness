# **THE BUSYNESS MIRROR** 

## **Are you building a High-Performance Organisation Or a BUSY one?** 

Most leaders know something is wrong before they can name it. You end a week having attended twelve meetings, replied to hundreds of messages, resolved three escalations, and still can't quite name what actually moved forward. 

BUSYness gives the illusion of productivity: calendars full and people in action. This is not random. BUSYness follows a predictable cycle, one that repeats until someone has the clarity and the courage to break it. 

It begins with Unclarity, an unclear purpose, strategy, or set of priorities. Without clarity, people default to Overactivity: reacting to everything that feels important

That overactivity spreads the organisation thin; time, energy, and resources are diluted across too many things simultaneously. The result is shallow results: a lot of motion but little meaningful progress. That gap between effort and outcome produces frustration, exhaustion, dissatisfaction, and a creeping sense that the work is not worth what it costs. And what do leaders and teams reach for when frustrated and uncomfortable? More activity. More BUSYness. The cycle starts again.

## **1.a Blind Spots of the BUSY Organisation** 

BUSYness rarely announces itself. Watch for these signs: 

## **1. Meetings run on visibility, not decisions.** 

People sit in rooms for hours, giving updates, presenting, discussing, arguing, managing egos, and surviving. At the end of those hours, though, the question, “What did we impact?” becomes an uncomfortable question; the meeting felt productive, but produced no concrete decisions and no clear actions. 

In a BUSY organisation, meetings consume significant time while generating few decisions that justify their cost. 

## **1.b Follow-up is the operating rhythm.** 

In our research across client organisations, senior leaders spend eight to twelve-hours every week chasing updates, clarifying missed commitments, and extracting status across email, WhatsApp, Slack, calls, and meetings. The

follow-up message has become such a normal part of the workflow that nobody questions it anymore. 

According to one HBR study (2017), managers spend 5.8 hours every week in unnecessary meetings. 

Across organisations, 53% of working time is now spent on what's called “busywork”, activity that feels productive but generates no meaningful output (Asana, 2024). 

In one of our in-house sessions in a Delhi-HQ organisation, the conference room held thirty leaders: the CEO, department heads, and senior managers of a 20,000person company. All perpetually busy. We put one question in front of them: “What percentage of your time is spent on internal follow-ups?” 

The responses were shocking. The average response: 60– 70% of the entire organisation's time is spent chasing work. Not creating value, but managing the failure to create value on the first commitment. 

**_If your organisation needs follow-up to move, you have Dangerous BUSYness Syndrome._** 

## **1.c Standards dropped without warning** 

Slowly, one compromise at a time, under genuine pressure from real circumstances, the bar has dropped. The worst part is that most people in the organisation don't even realise the bar has moved. The new lower standard has simply become normal.

## **1.d People just come to work, not to full a Purpose.** 

There is a difference, and you can feel it. When people are part of a real purpose, a cause they believe in, they show up differently. There is an energy of being engrossed in creating a future. When that's absent, people are professionally competent and spiritually hollow. They are doing a job, and they will do it until a better opportunity arrives. 

## **1.e The same problems keep coming back.** 

You solve a problem in Q1; it reappears in Q3 in a slightly different form. You address a team conflict; the same conflict is back three months later with different people. 

**_A BUSY organisation is very good at treating symptoms and very poor at addressing the systems that keep producing them._**
